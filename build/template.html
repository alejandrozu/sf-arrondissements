Les Arrondissements de San Francisco

The idea

San Francisco has more than a hundred neighborhoods, many only a few blocks wide, plus countless cultural districts and regions. Nobody can keep them all in their head. The arrondissements, inspired by those of Paris, add one layer above them: easy-to-manage districts bounded by the big avenues everyone already knows.

The rings

The arrondissements are organized into rings, a fan that sweeps north, then south, then east. There are three rings, and they grow larger as they move away from the center. San Francisco is far less dense than Paris, so its arrondissements are bigger.

Your neighborhoods

The neighborhoods have been adjusted to this new layout and mapped in detail. But you are what makes the city’s neighborhoods great, so your ideas for more neighborhoods and more labels are always welcome.

Each arrondissement has its own color and name, but they are best referred to by number.

                Arrondissements

                  The 15 arrondissements

                  Three rings
                    • Ring 11 → 3, north to south to east
                    • Ring 24 → 8 the same way, then 9 at the center
                    • Ring 310 → 15 the same way

                    Every ring is numbered in the same direction: it starts in the north, sweeps south, and ends in the east. Once one ring is learned, the other two follow. The Hills, which rise in the middle of the city, close the second ring as the 9th.

                    The rings follow the city itself, which fans out southwest from the Embarcadero. The first ring is the most urban and has the smallest arrondissements; the second is still dense; the third is mostly residential and has the largest. Population density falls the same way, with the Hills and Federal as the exceptions. The lowest numbers therefore go to the busiest districts, much as the Paris arrondissements spiral outward from the Louvre.

                    How it works

                    Names and numbers

                    As in Paris, the number carries the glory. Each arrondissement also has a short name, like Northport or The Valleys, but “the 7th” is the way to say it. Neighborhood names stay as they are; the arrondissement is the coarse address you give a friend.

                    Named for the hills

                    Where an arrondissement holds a prominent hill of its own, apart from the great heights at the center of the city, it takes that hill’s name: Pacific for Pacific Heights, Ashbury for Ashbury Heights, Potrero for Potrero Hill and Bayview for Bayview Hill. The central heights themselves, Twin Peaks, Mount Sutro and their neighbors, give the 9th its name, The Hills. A name taken from the land belongs to everyone who lives on it, so no single neighborhood is favored over the others.

                    The 1st is the one exception. It holds two celebrated hills, Telegraph Hill and Russian Hill, and in fairness to both it bears neither name: it is Northport.

                    The lines

                    Boundaries follow big, memorable streets: Market, Van Ness, Geary, Broadway, Masonic, Fulton and Lincoln, 19th Avenue, Sloat, Portola, Cesar Chavez, Mission Street, US-101 and I-280. Where the city ends, the water and the county line do the work.

                    Neighborhoods

                    The city’s neighborhood maps were redrawn to fit inside the arrondissements. Where a border cut a neighborhood in two, each side got its own name. Smaller places, like cultural districts and micro-neighborhoods, float on the map as labels without borders.

                    Colors

                    Fifteen colors: eleven hues of the color wheel plus brown, viridian, aubergine and black. Each is tied to something the place is known for.

                    Land for everyone

                    Golden Gate Park belongs to the whole city and sits outside the numbering. Every other piece of land, from the Presidio to Lake Merced, belongs to an arrondissement.
